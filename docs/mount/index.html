Camera + Jetson Mount — Design Brief

A 3D-printed mount holding the ZED X + Jetson AGX Orin on the Husky A200, for the VSLAM-vs-LiDAR study. A living document: every provisional number is replaced from your CAD drawings and the real robot before you cut a hole.

⚠ Confirm before you CAD

1  Overview

This mount is a 3D-printed structure that rigidly fixes a ZED X stereo camera and holds the Jetson AGX Orin Developer Kit on the top deck of a Clearpath Husky A200, so the robot can run a VSLAM-vs-LiDAR comparison. Its four jobs, in priority order: (1) hold the camera so rigidly that it does not flex, sag, or vibrate relative to the wheels — VSLAM treats the camera as bolted to the robot at an exact pose, and any wobble becomes map error; (2) place the camera at the exact pose the software expects, which as of 2026-08-29 is no longer a guess but a MEASURED as-built geometry: the mount point sits at x=0.081 m forward, y=-0.040 m (40 mm to the robot's RIGHT of centre), z=0.547 m above base_link, with 0.0565 rad (3.24 deg) of nose-down tilt, and the camera's LEFT LENS — the point the software actually reasons about — therefore lands at x=0.071, y=+0.020, z=0.563 relative to base_link, which is 0.6953 m above the FLOOR. (base_link is the robot's coordinate origin, at the midpoint of the drive-wheel axle projected to the ground; it sits 0.13228 m above the floor, so it is NOT the floor — see section 7 for why the two numbers differ and why the lens is 60 mm from the mount.) These SUPERSEDE the old x=0.35 / y=0.00 / z=0.62 / pitch=0.087 placeholders, which were never measured and describe no bracket that exists; (3) keep the Jetson cool (its fan pulls air in the top and pushes it out the sides — never blocked) and its connectors reachable; and (4) route the GMSL2 coax and power cables without kinking them past their bend radius. CRITICAL: almost every camera and Jetson dimension below is a PROVISIONAL published/reference figure. Before cutting a single bracket hole, replace them with numbers taken from your own ZED X and Jetson CAD/STEP models and measured off the actual robot — the Husky grid, the camera hole patterns, the Jetson hole pattern, and the cable bend radius are all unconfirmed for this specific unit.

5  Design constraints

The requirements the mount must satisfy — in priority order, rigidity and correct camera pose come first.

Camera field of view & mount tilt
Realize a 3.24 deg (0.0565 rad) downward optical-axis tilt and a level roll; nothing on the mount (arms, cable clamp, riser edges) may intrude into the ZED X's field of view, which for our 4.6 mm lens is 73 deg horizontal x 45 deg vertical x 87 deg diagonal [Stereolabs datasheet Rev 1.6, ZED X 4.6mm column, SKU ZED-312110]. CORRECTED 2026-08-29: this said "110 deg-class", which is the 2.2 mm wide lens — a different camera. Designing clearance to 110 deg would have over-constrained the mount against an obstruction that cannot occur. MEASURED 2026-08-29 on the as-built rig, superseding the earlier ~5 deg (0.087 rad) placeholder — see section 7.
Why: The tilt is deliberate: level at 0.695 m (the measured left-lens height above the floor) the camera leaves a blind zone ahead of the bumper (45 deg vertical field of view, 1 m minimum depth). CORRECTED 2026-08-29: this said 52 deg, which exceeds the datasheet maximum of 45 and so was wrong at every resolution. The narrower real figure makes the blind zone larger, not smaller — the first ground return sits 1.44 m ahead of the lens, not 1.24 m — so this argument for tilting the camera down is stronger than it was written, not weaker. Too little tilt and the near sidewalk is blind; too much and the frame fills with ground texture and starves SLAM of distant features. The mount is what physically sets this angle, so it must hit it repeatably.
Rigidity / vibration control
The camera-to-chassis load path must be stiff enough that the camera does not deflect, sag, or resonate under driving vibration and thermal expansion. Prefer a short, braced, triangulated path; avoid a tall unsupported cantilever mast.
Why: VSLAM assumes the camera is rigidly fixed at a known pose. Any flex or oscillation reads as real robot motion and corrupts the trajectory and map. Angular error dominates: a 1 deg wobble displaces a point at 20 m by ~35 cm. A wobbly mount silently ruins the exact far-field data the VSLAM-vs-LiDAR comparison depends on.
Jetson thermal / airflow
Leave the top fan intake unobstructed with generous clearance above it and keep the side exhaust paths open; do not enclose the Jetson in a sealed box; account for warm ambient near the deck.
Why: The dev-kit fan pulls air in the top and exhausts out the sides. Throttling it causes the Orin to thermal-throttle, which drops the compute the VSLAM pipeline needs. Airflow direction is an observed behaviour, not an NVIDIA spec, so design conservatively.
GMSL2 coax bend radius
Route the camera coax and the DC/USB cables with smooth curves that never kink; include a service loop and strain relief at the connector. Size the channel to the vendor's real min bend radius, NOT to a placeholder.
Why: A single locking FAKRA coax carries both power and data over-coax; kinking it degrades the signal or breaks the link. Stereolabs publishes no bend radius, so the channel must be sized from the actual cable spec once you have it — leave margin until then.
Power / connector clearance & access
Keep the ZED X rear connector, the Jetson rear I/O panel, the DC barrel jack, USB-C, RJ45, and the PCIe cover reachable with cables plugged; leave finger/tool room for insertion and removal.
Why: Cables must seat fully and be swappable without dismantling the mount. Blocking a connector or its strain relief forces sharp cable bends or makes the port unusable.
Serviceability
The camera and Jetson should each be removable, and the microSD slot, PCIe cover, and power/recovery/reset buttons accessible, without fully disassembling the mount or unbolting it from the Husky.
Why: This is a research rig that will be re-tilted, re-flashed, and re-cabled repeatedly (pitch is a Phase-4 study variable — but studying it means physically re-shimming the bracket and re-measuring, never editing the number, so the mount must make re-tilting and re-measuring practical). The microSD is flagged as reliability-suspect, so it must be swappable in the field.
Weather exposure
Assume outdoor use: the ZED X is IP66, but the Jetson AGX Orin Developer Kit is NOT weatherproof. Shield the Jetson and connectors from rain, sun, and dust, while still allowing its airflow (a vented/louvred cover, not a sealed one).
Why: A sidewalk robot sees rain and direct sun. Water on the dev kit or its ports risks a short; direct sun plus fan starvation compounds heat. Protection and cooling are in tension and must be solved together.
Centre of gravity & payload limit
Mount the combined ~1.5-2 kg payload low and near the deck centre-line; avoid a tall top-heavy mast. Stay well within the 75 kg max / 20 kg all-terrain payload.
Why: Mass is not the constraint (payload margin is huge), but a high CoG hurts tip stability on the 30 deg grades and turns-in-place the all-terrain rating warns about. Low and centred also keeps the camera pose stable under acceleration.

6  3D-print design rules

  • Wall thickness & perimeters. Use 3-4 mm minimum walls and at least 4 solid perimeter/shell lines on any load-bearing feature. Thin walls flex, and flex on a camera mount becomes map error. Load-critical zones (around inserts, at the base flange) should approach solid.
  • Ribs and gussets, not bulk. Stiffen with triangulated ribs and gussets at every corner where the camera arm meets the base, rather than one thick slab. Triangles resist bending; a printed cantilever without gussets will creep and vibrate. This buys stiffness without heavy, warp-prone thick sections.
  • Heat-set brass inserts for all threads. For the M3 (camera bottom), M4 (camera rear), and M2.5 (Jetson) fastening, install heat-set brass threaded inserts — do NOT tap threads directly into plastic. Plastic threads strip after a few service cycles; brass inserts give reusable metal threads and even clamp load. Use metal fasteners into the Husky's metal grid on the robot side.
  • Print orientation vs. load direction. Orient parts so the main load pulls ACROSS the layers, never straight along the Z (layer-adhesion) direction — printed layers split apart far more easily in Z than filament breaks in-plane. For a bracket carrying a downward/forward camera load, lay it so bending stress runs along the print lines, and add a wide base flange in the print plane.
  • Material choice — PETG/ASA, not PLA. Do NOT use PLA: it softens and creeps in direct sun and near the warm Jetson (glass transition ~55-60 C), so a PLA mount can sag and lose the camera pose on a hot day. Use PETG or ASA for an outdoor robot; ASA/ABS resist UV best, and glass-fibre-filled nylon (PA-CF/PA-GF) is the stiffest, most heat-stable option for the load-bearing camera arm if available.
  • Infill & solid load paths. Use high infill (40-60%+) or fully solid material along the direct load path from camera to deck, with generous top/bottom layers. Sparse infill under an insert or along a cantilever lets the part flex and the insert pull out. Density can drop only in non-structural filler regions.

7  The TF link — geometry must match the software

MEASURED 2026-08-29 — this section no longer states design targets. The mount's geometry IS the camera pose, and that pose has now been measured on the as-built rig rather than assumed. The six numbers in robot_frames.yaml (the camera_front block) are the rigid-body transform (the fixed position-and-rotation relationship between two points on a solid object) from base_link to the ZED X LEFT-lens frame, in ROS REP-103 convention (x forward, y left, z up; roll/pitch/yaw about x/y/z; metres and radians). base_link sits at the midpoint of the drive-wheel axle, projected to the ground — and it is 0.13228 m ABOVE the floor, so "relative to base_link" and "above the floor" are two different readings of the same z and differ by 13 cm. Every depth point the ZED SDK (the camera manufacturer's software library) reports is expressed in the left lens's optical frame, and SLAM and the costmap multiply each point by this transform to place it in the world — so these numbers are the sole bridge between what the camera sees and where the robot thinks things are.

The as-built geometry, and what each number is physically. Two different points are involved and they must not be confused. The MOUNT point (the frame the bracket physically creates, called zedx_base_frame in the robot model and zedx_front_base_link by the camera driver) sits at x=0.081, y=-0.040, z=0.547 relative to base_link, with roll=0, pitch=0.0565 rad, yaw=0. The LEFT LENS — which is what robot_frames.yaml records, because that is where the ZED SDK reports depth from — sits at x=0.071, y=+0.020, z=0.563, which is 0.6953 m above the floor. The difference between them is the camera's own internal base-to-lens offset, (-0.010, +0.060, +0.016), read from the live transform tree. How the bracket realizes each: how far forward the mount plate sits above the axle sets x; its left-right position sets y; the riser height sets z; and the tilt built into the arm sets pitch.

The lateral question is now ANSWERED by the as-built rig. The 163 mm housing with a 120 mm stereo baseline (the gap between the two lenses) puts the left lens about 60 mm to the robot's LEFT of the housing centre. The earlier version of this brief posed that as an open decision: offset the housing ~60 mm RIGHT so the lens lands on the centreline, or centre the housing and accept a ~+0.06 m lens offset. Neither was done. The as-built rig offsets the housing 40 mm to the robot's RIGHT (y=-0.040 at the mount), which leaves the left lens 20 mm to the robot's LEFT (y=+0.020). A replacement bracket does not have to reproduce that choice — but whatever it does, the 60 mm mount-to-lens gap does not go away, and the two numbers must be kept as two numbers. Copying the mount's y into robot_frames.yaml would bake in a 60 mm error while looking like a tidy-up.

How it was measured, so the claim can be audited. The height came from a tape reading of 0.7112 m from the floor to the ZED X housing UPPER SURFACE, then arithmetic: minus 0.0159 m (half the 31.8 mm body height, Stereolabs datasheet Rev 1.6) gives 0.6953 m for the camera centre above the floor; minus 0.0160 m (the camera's internal base-to-centre offset, read from the live transform tree) gives 0.6793 m for the mount frame; minus 0.13228 m (base_link above the floor, from the URDF's base_footprint joint) gives z=0.547. PRECISION CAVEAT: the datasheet gives the cross-section as 31.8 x 36.7 mm but never says which is height. 31.8 is taken as height (slim bar; 36.7 is lens-barrel depth). Reversed, z would be 0.5446 — 2.5 mm lower. Every other term is measured, so that 2.5 mm is the only assumption in the chain. The pitch came from a RANSAC floor-plane fit (a fitting method that finds the plane the largest number of points agree on and discards the rest, so chair legs and walls do not drag the answer): 5 frames, ~16,500 inliers each, reading -3.24 deg (spread 0.28) before the correction and -0.03 deg (spread 0.27) after, against a stated +/-0.3 deg noise floor; the reconstructed floor went from rising 226 mm over 4 m to rising 2 mm. That fit alone cannot say WHERE the angle comes from, because base_footprint is bolted to the robot body rather than to the world, so it reports the SUM of camera tilt, body pitch on the wheels, and the local floor patch's slope. The cause was settled by an independent accelerometer cross-check (the camera's internal motion sensor; at rest it measures gravity, an absolute vertical reference that owes nothing to the transform tree): 600 samples with the robot parked read 0.90 deg from vertical, where a level camera on a sloped floor patch would have predicted ~3.2 deg and a body pitched on its own wheels ~6.5 deg. Both excluded — the camera really is tilted in its mount.

Roll and yaw are 0, but that is "nobody has measured this", not "measured and found square". Roll: the plane fit read +0.24 deg (inside its own noise floor) and gravity read -1.37 deg; they disagree by more than either is worth, which sits within the combined sensor bias, the factory camera-to-sensor calibration, and a plausibly ~1 deg lab floor. Do not average them. Yaw is unmeasurable by both methods — a rotation about vertical leaves a horizontal plane horizontal and leaves gravity unchanged — and needs a known straight edge, or a driven straight line compared against heading. So the mount should still be built square in roll and yaw; there is simply no measurement backing the claim that the current one is.

Why a mount that disagrees with these numbers fails SILENTLY — which is more dangerous than a crash, and is the reason this section is long. A constant translation error (x/y/z) shifts the whole point cloud by a fixed offset: obstacles land a few cm off, and a z error makes flat ground read at the wrong height (looking like a low obstacle or a phantom drop-off) — but a translation error CANNOT tilt flat ground into a slope. Tilting the reconstructed ground into a ramp is an ANGULAR error: a pitch mismatch tips the whole scene, and because angular error scales with range (1 deg is ~35 cm at 20 m but only 1.7 cm at 1 m), a tilt you cannot see by eye is invisible up close yet severe exactly at the far distances relied on for early obstacle detection. Note: robot_frames.yaml's "35 m range" comment is optimistic (ZED X depth is ~20 m and the costmap only marks obstacles to 6 m), but the far-field argument holds regardless.

What robot_frames.yaml's measured: flag does and does not cover. That file's top-level measured: false is scoped to the base: block — the CHASSIS footprint, which is still manufacturer spec and has never been tape-checked on this unit. It does NOT speak for the camera. The camera_front block carries its own measured: true, measured_date: "2026-08-29" and a measured_method string beside the numbers it describes. Do not read the top-level false and conclude the camera pose is a guess, and do not flip it to true because the camera was measured — that would claim a chassis measurement nobody has taken.

What this means for the CAD, and what to do if the new bracket changes the pose. Treat the mount-point figures above as the geometry the replacement mount should REALIZE — they are what the running system currently believes. If the new bracket deliberately puts the camera somewhere else, that is allowed, but it is a re-measurement job, not an edit: measure the new pose with BOTH methods above (the plane fit for the angle, the gravity check for the cause), then update all FOUR files that state the pose, which live in this repository at catkin_ws/src/ (on the Jetson: ~/catkin_ws/src/): sidewalk_perception/launch/zedx_front.launch (cam_pos_x/y/z, cam_roll/pitch/yaw — drives the camera driver's own frame tree, which is what SLAM uses), sidewalk_bringup/urdf/husky_a200_real.urdf (the zedx_base_joint origin — the real robot's model), sidewalk_sim/urdf/husky_real.urdf (the same joint, kept identical — the simulation), and sidewalk_bringup/config/robot_frames.yaml (camera_front — the LEFT LENS, so mount plus the internal offset, NOT a copy of the mount). Then verify on the Jetson with the camera running: rosrun tf tf_echo zedx_base_frame zedx_front_base_link, which must read ~[0,0,0] translation and ~[0,0,0] rotation. A non-zero reading is the amount by which the robot model and the driver disagree; a pitch reading of about 0.0565 rad means one side has the tilt and the other does not, and about 0.113 rad means it got applied twice. Full procedure in docs/OPERATIONS.md, section "Editing the ZED X camera's position".
