Phase 3 · sidewalk_slam

Visual SLAM — Teaching the Robot to Draw Its Own Map

The robot builds a map of a place it has never seen, works out where it is on that map, and three different methods are made to compete on exactly the same data.

Ready to run

◆ In plain language

Imagine being led blindfolded into an unfamiliar building, then told to draw a floor plan and mark where you are standing. You cannot do one without the other. To draw the plan you need to know where you were when you saw each wall; to know where you are you need a plan to be somewhere on. This chicken-and-egg problem is the whole of what this package solves, and it has a name in robotics — SLAM, Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping.

The robot's only real sense is a stereo camera, a pair of eyes 12 centimetres apart. From those two slightly different views it works out how far away things are, exactly the way your own two eyes do. As it drives, it stitches those measurements together into a map, and by watching how the view shifts it works out how far it has moved.

The catch is that this stitching is never perfect. Each small error joins onto the last one, so after a few hundred metres the robot's idea of where it is has quietly slid away from the truth. The cure is recognition. When the robot comes back to a place it has been before and recognises it, it knows that two points it thought were far apart are actually the same point, and it can pull the whole map back into shape. That moment of recognition is called a loop closure, and watching one happen on screen is the single most satisfying thing in this project.

This package does that job three different ways and makes them race. One is the open research tool this project is built on, one is the commercial software that came with the camera, and one is a well-known academic system. All three are fed exactly the same recording, so any difference in their answers is a difference in the methods and not in the conditions. Comparing them fairly is the actual research contribution here.

Why this exists

A delivery robot on a sidewalk has to answer one question continuously and correctly — *where am I?* GPS is not good enough. In a city it bounces off buildings and can be wrong by five to ten metres, which on a two-metre-wide sidewalk is the difference between the pavement and the road. Under a bridge or beside a tall building it can be wrong by much more, or simply absent.

So the robot has to work it out from what it can see. That is what this package is for.

There is a second reason, and for a research project it is the more important one. Claiming that a method works is cheap. Showing that it works *better than the alternatives, on identical data, measured the same way* is what makes a result. This package is built so that the comparison is structurally fair: one recording, replayed three times, one output format, one validation standard. Everything else in the package exists to protect that fairness from the many small ways it can be quietly lost.

The physical picture

Loop closure is closing a surveying traverse.

A surveyor running a traverse measures from station to station around a circuit and comes back to the start. The final measurement almost never lands exactly on the starting peg. The gap is the misclosure, and it is the accumulated total of every small angular and distance error around the loop. The surveyor does not throw the survey away. They distribute the misclosure back through the legs — proportionally to length, or by a least-squares adjustment — so the circuit closes and every station moves a little.

That is precisely what happens when a SLAM system closes a loop. The robot drives a circuit, recognises the place it started from, and discovers that its own dead reckoning says it is four metres away from where it obviously is. That four metres is the misclosure. The pose graph optimiser then redistributes the error backwards through every pose in the loop, weighted by how confident each measurement was. The map visibly snaps into shape. Same idea, same mathematics, done sixty times a minute by a computer.

This is also why driving in a straight line and stopping produces a bad map no matter how good the algorithm is. A traverse that never closes has no misclosure to distribute, and therefore no way to correct itself.

Why two eyes give real distances and one eye cannot.

The two ZED X sensors are 120 mm apart. That separation is a physical ruler machined into the camera body. A point in the world projects into slightly different places in the two images, and that difference — the disparity — is linked to distance by simple similar triangles: distance equals focal length times baseline, divided by disparity. Because the baseline is a known length in millimetres, the answer comes out in millimetres. The system has metric scale, for free, from geometry.

A single camera cannot do this, ever, and not for want of cleverness. A photograph of a small object nearby and a large object far away can be pixel-for-pixel identical. Scale information is simply not present in the image. A monocular system recovers the *shape* of a trajectory correctly but can only guess its size, and that guess drifts. This is why every serious ground robot uses stereo, a depth sensor, an IMU, or wheel odometry — anything that puts a real length into the problem.

Where the accuracy goes as things get further away.

Depth uncertainty for a stereo pair grows as the square of range — roughly z² divided by focal length times baseline. The ZED X datasheet says the same thing in plain numbers: error under 0.4% out to 2 metres, and under 7% at 20 metres. (Until 2026-08-29 this page said "about 0.2% at 1 metre, about 3.1% at 15 metres" and attributed that to the datasheet. Those two figures appear nowhere in it; they had been written from memory and then repeated across several files as though the manufacturer had stated them.) Doubling the distance roughly quadruples the uncertainty. That single fact drives several settings in this package: features beyond about 15 metres are not trusted for pose estimation, and the occupancy grid is built only from points within 8 metres. Far-away points do not add information; they add smooth, plausible-looking error.

And there is a floor as well as a ceiling. This camera returns nothing valid closer than 1 metre. There is a metre-deep blind shell around the robot at all times. That is a mechanical design constraint, not a software one: it means a curb the robot is about to touch is invisible, and any close-quarters obstacle avoidance will need another sensor.

Why the camera bracket is a SLAM component.

Everything above assumes the two lenses stay exactly 120 mm apart and exactly parallel. A bracket that flexes by a tenth of a degree under vibration changes the epipolar geometry, and every disparity measured while it is flexed is wrong. The same applies to the IMU, which sits a few centimetres from the lens: rotating about a point offset by a lever arm produces a centripetal acceleration the accelerometer feels and the camera does not. Get that offset wrong by two centimetres and, at a modest one radian per second of turn, you have injected a constant 0.02 m/s² error that is integrated twice into position. Stiffness and accurate mounting measurements are not tidiness here. They are accuracy.

What it actually does

  1. Runs RTAB-Map as the primary mapping system, fed with the ZED X depth images, rectified colour and the camera's 200 Hz IMU
  2. Produces both a flat 2D occupancy grid for navigation and a full 3D point cloud map, from the same run
  3. Saves everything into one reloadable database file, and offers a separate mode that localises on an existing map without changing it
  4. Detects the RTAB-Map core library already installed on the Jetson and builds only the missing ROS layer against it, instead of installing a second conflicting copy
  5. Records the ZED SDK's own built-in position tracking as a commercial baseline
  6. Generates the ORB-SLAM3 configuration file from the camera's live calibration, so the numbers in it can never be out of date
  7. Runs all three systems one after another over the same recording, and writes all three answers in one common trajectory format
  8. Checks every result for physical impossibility before anyone is allowed to treat it as evidence
  9. Refuses to touch the live robot until a recorded run has passed those checks

Key ideas, explained twice

Once in everyday words, once in the technical terms you will meet in the literature.

SLAM
PlainlyBuilding a map of somewhere you have never been while simultaneously working out where you are on the map you are still building. Each half depends on the other, which is why it is hard.
TechnicallySimultaneous Localisation and Mapping. Joint estimation of a sensor trajectory and an environment representation, usually posed as maximum a posteriori inference over a factor graph.
Odometry
PlainlyEstimating how far you have moved by comparing what you can see now to what you saw a moment ago. It works well over short distances and drifts steadily over long ones, because every estimate is measured from the previous estimate.
TechnicallyIncremental ego-motion estimation. Error is compounded rather than bounded, so uncertainty grows monotonically with distance travelled.
Drift
PlainlyThe slow, invisible accumulation of small errors. Nothing looks wrong at any moment; after two hundred metres the robot is confidently in the wrong place.
TechnicallyUnbounded growth of pose covariance in the absence of loop closures or absolute measurements.
Loop closure
PlainlyRecognising somewhere you have already been, realising your accumulated error, and correcting the whole path backwards to fit. Exactly like closing a surveying traverse and distributing the misclosure.
TechnicallyDetection of a revisit, insertion of a constraint between non-consecutive poses, and global pose-graph optimisation to redistribute the residual.
Pose graph
PlainlyA skeleton of the journey where each joint is somewhere the robot was and each bone is a measured movement between two of them. Correcting the map means gently reshaping the skeleton until all the bones fit at once.
TechnicallyA graph whose vertices are SE(3) or SE(2) poses and whose edges are relative transform constraints with information matrices, solved by nonlinear least squares (g2o, GTSAM, Ceres).
Metric scale
PlainlyKnowing that a distance in the map is really that many metres, and not just that many arbitrary units. Two cameras a known distance apart give you this for free. One camera never can.
TechnicallyObservability of absolute scale. Recovered from a calibrated stereo baseline, a depth sensor, or from IMU accelerations after sufficient excitation. Unobservable in a pure monocular system.
Occupancy grid
PlainlyThe map as graph paper. Every square is marked free, blocked, or unknown. This is the simple flat map the navigation system actually plans routes on.
TechnicallyA discretised 2D probabilistic map, published as nav_msgs/OccupancyGrid, with cell values as occupancy probability in the range 0 to 100 and -1 for unknown.
TUM trajectory format
PlainlyOne line per position, listing the time, three numbers for where, and four for which way it was facing. Chosen because everything can read it and because the earlier work on this project already used it.
Technicallytimestamp tx ty tz qx qy qz qw, whitespace separated, quaternion scalar-last. Directly consumable by evo and by the standard ATE/RPE tooling.
Localisation mode
PlainlyUsing a map you already have without adding to it. The robot answers only where am I, and the map is left untouched, so two runs over the same route are properly comparable.
TechnicallyRTAB-Map with Mem/IncrementalMemory disabled and the working memory pre-loaded, performing appearance-based relocalisation against a fixed graph.
Simulated time
PlainlyWhen replaying a recording, every program is told to use the clock from the recording instead of the real clock. Without this, two replays of the same file produce results with different timestamps that cannot be lined up.
Technicallyuse_sim_time with /clock published by rosbag play --clock; all node time queries resolve to recorded time.

Inputs and outputs

Takes in

  • Rectified colour images from the ZED X, on the camera's rgb/image_rect_color topic
  • Registered depth images, aligned to the colour camera, in metres
  • Camera calibration on camera_info — the intrinsics, read live and never hardcoded
  • IMU data at 200 Hz from inside the camera, used to hold roll and pitch level against gravity (this was recorded as 400 Hz until 2026-08-29; 400 Hz is the older ZED 2's figure, and the ZED X datasheet states 200 Hz in two separate tables)
  • The transform tree from sidewalk_bringup, which says where the camera sits relative to the robot base
  • Recorded SVO2 files or rosbags from sidewalk_perception, for offline runs
  • config/rtabmap_zedx.yaml — every tuning decision, in one commented file

Produces

  • A 2D occupancy grid on /rtabmap/grid_map, saveable as the pgm and yaml pair that navigation loads
  • A 3D point cloud map on /rtabmap/cloud_map, exportable to a .ply file
  • One RTAB-Map database per session holding the entire map, reloadable for localisation
  • The corrected map to odom transform, which is the robot's live position estimate
  • Three trajectories per comparison run, in the common TUM format, ready for the evaluation package
  • A run manifest recording what was run over what, with which settings
  • A validation verdict that fails the run if anything is physically impossible
  • A timestamped log per execution in logs/sidewalk_slam/

How to run it

python3 catkin_ws/src/sidewalk_slam/scripts/slam_common.py

Start here. Prints what this machine actually has installed — ROS, the RTAB-Map core and its version, which rtabmap_ros packages exist, the ZED SDK, ORB-SLAM3. It only reads; it launches nothing. Safe to run anywhere, including on a computer without ROS.

rosrun sidewalk_slam build_rtabmap_ros.py

Works out how to build the missing ROS layer of RTAB-Map against the core library already installed on the Jetson, and writes the plan out as a commented shell script. It does NOT build anything until you add --execute. Read the plan first — it explains why installing rtabmap-ros from apt would break things.

roslaunch sidewalk_slam rtabmap_mapping.launch rtabmap_viz:=true

Build a new map. The camera must already be running. Turn the GUI on the first few times and watch the pose graph: the moment a loop closes and the map snaps into alignment is worth seeing before you read any more theory. Drive slowly and revisit places, or no loop will ever close.

rosrun sidewalk_slam map_manager.py save-grid --name lab_floor2

Save the live 2D grid as the pgm and yaml pair that the navigation package will load in Phase 5-6. Run it while mapping is still going; it captures the grid as it stands.

rosrun sidewalk_slam map_manager.py export --db ~/rtabmap_maps/sidewalk_front.db

Turn a finished map database into a 3D point cloud file you can open in MeshLab or CloudCompare. This is the map you put in a report.

rosrun sidewalk_slam map_manager.py prepare --db ~/rtabmap_maps/sidewalk_front.db --name lab_floor2

Make a protected copy of a map for localisation, so no mistyped argument can ever destroy the original. Making a map takes an afternoon; copying it takes a second.

roslaunch sidewalk_slam rtabmap_localization.launch database_path:=/home/USER/rtabmap_maps/lab_floor2_localization.db

Find yourself on a map you already have, without changing it. Start the robot somewhere it definitely mapped — until it recognises a place, it believes it is at the map origin, which looks deceptively like working.

rosrun sidewalk_slam make_orbslam3_config.py --from-topic --camera-name zedx_front --out ~/orbslam3_zedx.yaml --dump-json ~/zedx_calib.json

Write the ORB-SLAM3 settings file from the camera's live calibration. It measures the real stereo baseline from the calibration and checks it against the ZED X nominal 120 mm, refusing to continue if they disagree. The JSON dump lets you regenerate the file later on a machine with no camera attached.

roslaunch sidewalk_slam orbslam3_baseline.launch dry_run:=true

Check the ORB-SLAM3 baseline is actually runnable before you drive anywhere. Two seconds now, instead of finding out after the experiment that the vocabulary file was never extracted.

roslaunch sidewalk_slam slam_from_recording.launch bag:=/path/to/indoor_loop.bag

Replay a recording through RTAB-Map with the GUI on. This is the tuning loop — change a parameter, replay, compare, repeat, on data that never changes underneath you.

rosrun sidewalk_slam slam_runner.py --input /path/to/indoor_loop.bag --dry-run

Print exactly what the full three-way comparison would run, without running it. Works on any machine, including one with no ROS. Read this before the real thing.

rosrun sidewalk_slam slam_runner.py --input /path/to/indoor_loop.bag --settings ~/orbslam3_zedx.yaml --execute

The real experiment. Runs RTAB-Map, then the ZED SDK tracker, then ORB-SLAM3, one at a time, over the same recording with the recorded clock, and writes three trajectories plus a manifest. Takes about three times the length of the recording.

rosrun sidewalk_slam validate_slam_run.py --run-id 20260720-140322_compare --closed-loop

The gate. Checks every trajectory for backwards timestamps, NaNs, non-unit rotations, impossible speeds, tracking dropouts and short coverage, and compares the stacks for gross disagreement. Fails the run if anything is impossible. Nothing goes onto live hardware until this passes.

On the first real run, check with rtabmap --params | grep Grid, then delete whichever line was rejected and this warning goes away.
ORB-SLAM3 rejects the generated settings file, or reads zeros out of itThe settings-file schema is a genuine unknown. make_orbslam3_config.py emits the flat legacy Camera.fx layout by default because that is what the known-working ~/oak-d-params.yaml on this Jetson uses. ORB-SLAM3 master instead expects Camera1.* keys plus a File.version of 1.0. Which one the installed checkout wants has not been tested against the real binary.Rerun make_orbslam3_config.py with --schema v1 to emit the newer layout. That path converts the baseline from fx * baseline to plain metres and refuses to write a file if that conversion fails, but it has NOT been validated against the installed build — it prints a warning saying so. Treat the first ORB-SLAM3 run as a test of the schema, not as a result.
The ORB-SLAM3 stereo-inertial result looks plausible but you are asked how much to trust itThe IMU noise densities and random-walk terms in the generated settings are conservative estimates for a consumer-grade MEMS part, not measurements. This entry used to add that Stereolabs publishes the sensor's ranges and resolution but not its noise density — that was wrong, and it was corrected on 2026-08-29. The datasheet does publish both noise densities (accelerometer 2.3 mg, gyroscope 0.20 dps) and a sensitivity error of plus or minus 0.5 per cent, in the same small table as the resolution figures that were being quoted. What it does not publish is the bias instability or the random-walk terms. The published densities are still not used directly, for one specific reason: a noise density is normally quoted per square root of a hertz, and this datasheet prints no such denominator, so the figures could mean either the density itself or the total noise across the sensor's whole bandwidth — readings that differ by about a factor of ten. Putting a number of uncertain units into a setting whose units are fixed would be a worse error than the honest estimate it replaced.Say so in the report, but do not treat the characterisation as a blocker any more — the wrong version of this note made it one. Two things would settle it. The quick one: ask Stereolabs support which reading of the two density figures is intended; that is a short question with a short answer. The thorough one: leave the camera absolutely still on a solid bench for three or more hours, record the IMU topic, and run an Allan variance analysis (allan_variance_ros or kalibr_allan). The Allan variance run is still worth doing because it also delivers the bias instability and random-walk terms, which genuinely are unpublished — but a stereo-inertial result produced before it is indicative, not invalid. Separately, note that the IMU frequency written into the settings was itself wrong until 2026-08-29 (400 Hz declared for a 200 Hz sensor), and ORB-SLAM3 scales all four noise terms by the square root of that number, so any settings file generated before that date must be regenerated rather than believed. The stereo-only ORB-SLAM3 result does not depend on any of these values and is unaffected.
